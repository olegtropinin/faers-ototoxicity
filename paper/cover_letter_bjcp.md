Dear Editor,

I submit the original research article "Adjustment and external replication of drug–hearing-loss signals:
a disproportionality analysis of FAERS and Canada Vigilance" for consideration as an Original Article in the *British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology*.

This study addresses a methodological question relevant to routine pharmacovigilance: whether widely reported FAERS
hearing-loss signals survive bias adjustment and independent replication. Several recent studies screened FAERS for
drug-associated hearing loss with crude disproportionality and reported many unlabelled signals. Using 19.9 million deduplicated FAERS reports
and 1.2 million Canada Vigilance reports, the study compares crude and adjusted detection against a label-derived reference set,
replicates every signal in Canada Vigilance with a power-calibrated comparison, and reviews the report provenance of each
robust unlabelled signal, including an active-comparator check within the indication.

The main findings are methodological:

- adjustment for indication, ototoxic co-medication and stimulated reporting changes the signal list but does not
  improve agreement with labels;
- labelled hearing-loss signals replicate in Canada Vigilance about as often as their FAERS strength predicts
  (observed/expected 1.02), whereas unlabelled signals replicate less than half as often (0.44; 0.26 when both databases
  are adjusted);
- solicited patient-support-programme reports, report clusters and shared co-medication produce spurious replications,
  so a second spontaneous-reporting database is informative only when both estimates are adjusted and these sources are
  handled explicitly;
- after these steps, seven of 22 robust unlabelled signals remain (two to nine under alternative post-hoc choices), as hypotheses.

The analysis protocol was committed to a version-controlled repository before the first result was computed; every later
change, including all post-hoc analyses, is dated and labelled, and Figure 1 separates the pre-specified analyses from the
post-hoc ones. As BJCP requires for disproportionality analyses, the study follows READUS-PV and the completed checklist is
provided (Supplementary Table S1).

The main text is about 4,500 words, somewhat above the guide of 4,000 for Original Articles. The extra length comes from
reporting two databases, keeping pre-specified and post-hoc analyses apart, and describing the case-level review in enough
detail to be reproduced; I would be glad to shorten it if the editors prefer. The manuscript is submitted in free format. Code, protocol and result tables are publicly available (https://github.com/olegtropinin/faers-ototoxicity; doi:10.5281/zenodo.23659658).

The manuscript has not been published in a journal and is not under consideration elsewhere. A version of it is publicly
available together with the code (doi:10.5281/zenodo.23659658); it has not been posted on a preprint server.
There was no funding. Until October 2026 the author was employed as a software engineer at Suno, a company that develops practice-management software for hearing clinics; the company had no role in this study. AI tools were used in data processing,
analysis and drafting under the author's direction, as described in the Methods.
