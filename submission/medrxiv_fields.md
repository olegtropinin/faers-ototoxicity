**Title:** Adjustment and external replication of drug–hearing-loss signals: a disproportionality analysis of FAERS and Canada Vigilance

**Article type:** New Results · **Subject area:** Pharmacology and Therapeutics (alternative: Otolaryngology)

**Author:** Oleg Tropynin — Independent researcher, Karaganda, Kazakhstan — tropininoleg@gmail.com — ORCID 0009-0006-5431-4285 (corresponding)

**Keywords:** pharmacovigilance; FAERS; Canada Vigilance; disproportionality analysis; ototoxicity; hearing loss; tinnitus; signal detection; READUS-PV

**Abstract:**
Introduction: Recent analyses of the US Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) reported many drug–hearing-loss signals absent from labels. We assessed which survive adjustment for hearing-specific biases and external replication.
Methods: Disproportionality analysis of 19.9 million deduplicated FAERS reports (2004–2026) and 1.2 million Canada Vigilance reports, following a protocol committed before analysis. For hearing loss and tinnitus, crude reporting odds ratios (signal: lower 95% bound  > 1, ≥3 cases) were compared with Mantel–Haenszel estimates stratified by age, sex and period after excluding ear-related indications, reports naming established ototoxic drugs and lawyer reports. Methods were compared on a label-derived reference set (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, AUC) and by replication in Canada Vigilance; robust unlabelled signals underwent case-by-case review.
Results: Adjustment changed the hearing-loss signal list (63 of 300 crude signals lost) but not agreement with labels (AUC, crude vs adjusted lower bound: 0.718 vs 0.723). Unlabelled signals replicated in Canada more than background but less than labelled ones; power-calibrated (post hoc), labelled signals replicated as predicted (observed/expected 1.02, 95% CI 0.77–1.32) and unlabelled ones did not (0.44, 0.29–0.62), also within strata of consumer reporting. Solicited reports, report clusters and shared co-medication produced spurious replications. Of 22 robust unlabelled signals (post hoc), 15 had artefactual or known explanations; 7 remained (2–9 under alternative choices), led by pregabalin.
Conclusions: Most unlabelled FAERS hearing-loss signals do not survive adjusted replication and provenance review. Findings are hypothesis-generating; cross-database replication needs adjusted estimates and explicit handling of solicited reports.
